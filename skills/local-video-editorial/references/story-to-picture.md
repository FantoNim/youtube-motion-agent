# Story to picture

Modified editorial adaptation; sources and license are recorded in [SOURCES.md](../SOURCES.md).

## Build the argument before its decoration

1. State the viewer's situation, the question being answered and the one useful takeaway for this section. A long lesson can have several chapters; do not compress its whole subject into a 30-second pitch.
2. Separate source facts from creative choices. Trace numbers, interface claims and quotations to actual sources. Label hypothetical examples; do not turn rough estimates into measured results.
3. Prefer a worked example, a demonstrated process or a before/after that earns the claim. A polished card is not evidence.
4. Follow an existing script, approved reference or established house look. When the creative route is genuinely open, compare a few materially different approaches privately and recommend one; ask the user only where the choice matters. Do not force three-option approval gates onto ordinary editing.

## Match the information shape

| Spoken purpose | Useful visual | Avoid |
| --- | --- | --- |
| A person makes an argument | Their camera shot; restrained emphasis or one sourced example | Covering their face with constant cards |
| An interface action proves a step | Authorized screen recording; crop/highlight the actual control | An invented interface passed off as the real product |
| Before and after | Same subject with a clear state change | Unrelated stock clips as proof |
| Ordered process | One example progressing through visible steps | A finished complex diagram before its parts are introduced |
| Comparison | Aligned alternatives with the same criteria | Decorative rows with no meaningful difference |
| Quantity or change | A sourced number, simple bar/line or concrete scale | Invented precision, distorted axes or a chart just for movement |
| Parts or relationships | Labels attached to the part; a diagram that builds as named | Detached legends or misleading arrows |
| Conceptual metaphor | A recognizable, useful object or action | An abstraction that needs a second explanation |
| Chapter transition | Brief title/reset and a clear return to the subject | Reintroducing the whole brand each time |
| Reaction or uncertainty | Appropriate human expression or licensed avatar pose | Claiming phoneme-accurate animation from a static cut-out |

## Talking-head route

Keep the speaker visible when their expression or delivery carries the point. Use B-roll to show what they describe, not to fill a percentage quota. Hold a source screen long enough to understand its relevant part; protect names, keys and private account UI. Cutaways can cover an approved speech edit, but cannot repair a change in meaning. Use punch-ins sparingly and within source resolution; do not silently change aspect ratio or invent extra camera detail.

## Avatar route

Treat the character as a consistent presenter with a small reusable pose vocabulary. Reuse poses and natural holds; change pose when the rhetoric changes. Screens, examples and diagrams may take over entirely when they communicate better. A pose library, a layered rig and synchronized mouth animation are separate asset requirements. Do not extract a creator's character as a reusable production asset pack merely because their video was used as a reference.

## Timing and words

Use the actual voice as the clock. Reveal a diagram part or label near when it is named; leave enough time to read and understand it. On-screen text normally complements speech rather than duplicating a paragraph, except when captions are requested. A reading-time heuristic is a starting question, not an enforced edit: check the real string at its delivered size. Preserve deliberate pauses and demonstrations; do not impose a new visual every fixed number of seconds.

## Reusable scene grammar

Start with a small vocabulary: speaker/presenter; screen proof with callout; example/B-roll; title or chapter reset; comparison; process/relationship diagram. Save approved layout and motion settings in the project. Let the agent choose among these forms by content, then critique the rendered result. Do not make the user design a new preset for every sentence.
